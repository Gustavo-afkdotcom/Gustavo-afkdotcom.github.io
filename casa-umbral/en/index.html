The problem

A flat house in a mountain country

In Colombia, rural housing is designed as if the ground were flat and the climate did not matter. A bench is cut into the slope, block walls go up, and a fiber-cement roof goes on top. In Anapoima, that house, simulated over a full year, spends more than half of the time outside comfort, and it requires moving 397 m³ of earth and building a 3.15 m retaining wall.

Casa Umbral starts from two questions. Can architectural form be derived from the physics of the site: sun, air, water, soil and earthquakes? And can that form compete with the typical house in comfort and cost?

Beauty has to be a consequence of how the house works.

The idea

Three rooms that step down the slope

Instead of one bench, three terraces. Each one is a room with earth walls and a thermal garden beside it, under a roof lifted off the ceiling that gives shade and collects rain. The design rule: every element does at least two jobs.

Wall

Retains the soil and stores the night’s coolness for the afternoon.

Roof

Gives ventilated shade and harvests 171 m³ of rain per year.

Garden

Is circulation and a climatic space: strong heat stress drops from 48 % to 12 % of the hours.

Stair

Connects the terraces and is the channel that carries the hillside’s water.

Eave

Protects the west façade from the afternoon sun and keeps rain off the earth wall.

Excavation

Shapes the terraces and supplies the soil for the blocks: ~115 m³ come out, the walls use ~45.

From parametric model to construction set

The v16.1 base geometry was brought from Grasshopper into Revit with Dynamo. What the experiments found

Decisions that came from measuring

The roof decides

Replacing the ventilated roof with fiber cement attached to the room removes 3,373 hours of comfort. Shade comes from the drop of the eave, not from the number of folds: more folds only use more material.

Earthquakes drew the façade

The confined masonry walls required by NSR-10 (the Colombian seismic code) set the rhythm of the narrow openings. The pergola ended up solving privacy between rooms.

The optimizer sacrifices what it does not measure

NSGA-II found a better house, but it dropped privacy and shortened the eave to 0.65 m because durability was not one of its objectives. In v16.1 the eave went back to 0.95 m: it protects the earth wall and recovers 19 hours of comfort.

Tested and rejected

Lightweight bahareque (wattle-and-daub) end walls: they saved 6 million COP, but added 111 hours outside comfort. The mass of those walls does work, so they stayed in compressed earth block.

The optimizer finds the best within what it is asked for. Knowing what to ask is the architect’s job.

Limits

What remains to be tested

Everything is simulated; nothing is measured yet. The climate follows IDEAM climate normals, comfort is ASHRAE 55 adaptive over all 8,760 hours of the year, and no credit was given to fans or to air movement on the body. The cost balance relies on assumed prices for on-site blocks, the ferrocement cistern and the bamboo: the next step is to get real quotes. The site is a standard 30 % slope with an assumed soil study.

Casa Umbral is a method more than a house: with another climate and another site, the same 22 experiments would produce a different architecture.
